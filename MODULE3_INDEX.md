## A. Vuorela – *Development Setting and Methods* (Laurea slides)

- **Type:** Teaching material (not empirical research); covers research-based development work in Master's theses.
- **Core ideas:**
  - Three kinds of work are compared (Moilanen et al. 2022): scientific research (creates and tests theory),
    research-based development (solves practical problems, collects data systematically and evaluates it critically,
    works with stakeholders) and everyday development (random information gathering, little critical evaluation).
  - The *development setting* chapter covers: purpose, objectives, tasks and questions; methodological choices;
    data collection; the data management plan; and data analysis.
  - Approaches: case study, constructive research, action research, service design, foresight, innovation creation.
  - Six-step process: identify the target → gather practice and theory data → define the scope → literature review and method plan → implement → evaluate.
- **Methods content:**
  - *Mixed methods* examples: a consumer survey followed by employee interviews; or document analysis, then a
    workshop or interview with observation.
  - *Quantitative:* tests an objective theory through relationships between variables using statistics. Variables are
    predefined and numerically measurable. Emphasises generalisability and ruling out alternative explanations. Uses
    large samples, surveys, measurements and experimental vs control groups. Example: before/after customer-experience
    surveys compared statistically. The sample is assumed to represent the population, and a census studies the whole
    population. Surveys typically need **≥ 50 respondents**. A single survey is cross-sectional and descriptive;
    repeated surveys are longitudinal and support cause–effect assessment. Parametric tests need normally distributed
    data. Results are reported as distributions, frequencies, mean, mode and median, in bar charts, pie charts and tables.
  - *Qualitative:* maps people's understanding; questions and methods may change during the study; stresses
    individuality and diversity; participants talk relatively freely. Data comes from interviews (thematic or
    semi-structured is most common; also in-depth and focus group), texts, video and observation. Five-step process:
    plan → familiarise → collect → analyse → conclude/report.
  - *Content analysis* can be data-driven (most common in theses), theory-guided or theory-driven. Its three stages
    are reduction, clustering and abstraction. Other analysis types: typification, thematisation, quantification.
- **Efficacy findings:** none (not an empirical study).

## B. Bhattacherjee (2012) – *Social Science Research*

- **Type:** Graduate research-methods textbook, 16 chapters (no own empirical data).
- **Chapters (printed page):** 1 Science 1 · 2 Thinking like a researcher 9 · 3 Research process 17 · 4 Theories 25 ·
  **5 Research design 35** · 6 Measurement 43 · **7 Reliability & validity 55** · 8 Sampling 65 · 9 Survey 73 ·
  **10 Experimental 83** · 11 Case 93 · 12 Interpretive 103 · 13 Qualitative analysis 113 · 14 Descriptive stats 119 ·
  15 Inferential stats 129 · 16 Ethics 137. (PDF page = printed page + 9.)
- **High-yield definitions:**
  - *Research design* (p. 35) is the blueprint for data collection. It specifies three processes: data collection,
    instrument development and sampling.
  - *Positivist vs interpretive* (pp. 35, 38). Positivist methods (lab experiments, surveys) test theory deductively.
    Interpretive methods (action research, ethnography) build theory inductively. The book says it is **incorrect** to
    equate these with quantitative vs qualitative, which refers to the *type of data*. Mixed-mode designs that combine
    both kinds of data are "often highly desirable" (p. 35).
  - *Four validity attributes* (pp. 35–37):
    - **Internal validity** (causality) requires covariation, temporal precedence and no plausible alternative
      explanation.
    - **External validity** (generalisability) has two forms: population and ecological.
    - **Construct validity** asks whether the scale measures the intended construct.
    - **Statistical conclusion validity** asks whether the right test was used and its assumptions were met. It does
      not apply to interpretive research.
  - *Trade-offs* (pp. 36, 39). Lab experiments are high in internal and low in external validity. Field surveys are
    high in external and low in internal validity. Field experiments, longitudinal surveys and multiple case studies
    can be high on both (the "cone of validity").
  - *Controls* (pp. 37–38): manipulation, elimination, inclusion, statistical control and randomization.
    **Random selection** draws the sample from the population. **Random assignment** places subjects in groups.
  - *Experiments* (pp. 38–39). There is a treatment group and a control group, and a placebo can be used. The book's
    example is a drug-efficacy trial with random assignment. If subjects are not randomly assigned, the design is
    **quasi-experimental**. Experiments are the "gold standard" (p. 83).
  - *Field surveys* (p. 39). Cross-sectional surveys measure the independent and dependent variables at the same time.
    Longitudinal surveys measure the dependent variable later. Surveys are prone to social-desirability bias.
  - *Secondary data analysis* (p. 39) uses data that others collected for a different purpose.
  - *Reliability* (pp. 56–57): consistency of a measure. Types: inter-rater, test-retest, split-half and
    internal consistency.
  - *Sampling* (pp. 67, 69). In probability sampling every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection. In
    nonprobability sampling (quota, convenience, …) the chance is zero or unknown, so results cannot be generalised.
  - *Survey research* (p. 73): standardised questionnaires or interviews.
  - *Case research* (pp. 94–95) suits exploratory "how/why" questions. Sites are chosen by theoretical sampling. Data
    should be triangulated across interviews, observation, documents and archives.
  - *Interpretive research* (p. 103) assumes reality is shaped by human experience and social context.
  - *Content / sentiment analysis* (p. 116) classifies messages as positive, negative or neutral.
  - *Ethics* (p. 137–138): voluntary participation, anonymity vs confidentiality.
- **Efficacy findings:** none. The only "efficacy" example is the hypothetical drug trial on p. 38.

## C. Cárdenas (2019) – *Quantitative Analysis: The Guide for Beginners*

- **Type:** Beginner handbook on the quantitative research process, 11 chapters with exercises. Data comes from the
  World Values Survey and Latinobarómetro.
- **Key content (printed page):**
  - *Ch.1 (pp. 5–6)* compares the two methods:
    - **Quantitative:** positivism; measurable data; explanation; relationships; outcomes; generalisable if the sample
      is representative; many cases; statistical analysis.
    - **Qualitative:** phenomenology; experiences; comprehension; depth; process; transferable rather than
      generalisable; few cases; content analysis.

    Mixed methods example: numerical data first to select a population segment, then in-depth interviews.
  - *Ch.2–3 (pp. 7–15).* The problem must be concise, relevant, contextualised and specific. The research question
    must be concise, achievable and relevant. Three-step protocol plus six strategies: description, causes,
    consequences, solution, comparison by place, comparison by time. A hypothesis is a tentative answer to the
    research question.
  - *Ch.4 (pp. 17–20).* Operationalisation: hypothesis → define concepts → dimensions → indicators.
  - *Ch.5 (pp. 22–25).* Primary vs secondary sources. Reliability criteria for secondary sources. Pros: more data
    and comparisons. Cons: less control.
  - *Ch.6 (pp. 26–31).* Survey design and common question-writing errors (e.g. double-barrelled, negative, ambiguous).
    Likert scales (1932). Response categories must be exhaustive and mutually exclusive. Validation is external
    (experts) and internal (pilot).
  - *Ch.7 (pp. 32–35).* Representativeness (equitable vs equivalent criterion). Non-random sampling: convenience,
    quota, judgment, snowball, volunteer. Random sampling: simple, systematic, stratified, cluster. Sample-size factors:
    N, heterogeneity of 50%, error of 3–5%, confidence of 95%. Formula: n = N·Z²·p(1−p) / ((N−1)e² + Z²·p(1−p)).
  - *Ch.8 (pp. 36–38).* Variables can be nominal, ordinal or scale. Analysis can be univariate, bivariate or
    multivariate, each with its own techniques.
  - *Ch.9 (pp. 39–41).* Frequency tables, mean and standard deviation, histograms.
  - *Ch.10 (pp. 42–46).* Crosstabs: independent variable in columns, column percentages, chi-square.
  - *Ch.11 (pp. 47–50).* Pearson r. A result is significant if p < 0.05. Examples: ideology vs importance of God is
    r = 0.124, p = 0.000 (weak positive). Age vs interest in politics is r = 0.013, p = 0.467 (not significant).
- **Efficacy findings:** none.
