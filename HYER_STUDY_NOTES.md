## p. 202 – Abstract & Introduction

- **Why research matters:** it adds to the knowledge base, improves practice, shows which practices are effective and
  which are not, informs policy debates, and makes health professionals better practitioners. Findings are shared at
  scientific meetings, conferences and in journals.
- **To be useful, research must be:** carried out **ethically**, with **full disclosure of how the data were
  collected**, and analysed with **appropriate statistical methods**. Only then can valid conclusions be drawn and
  practice changed.
- **Aim of the paper:** explain the differences between the two approaches and which suits *your* planned research.
- **Narrowing a topic.** A broad topic such as "The health of teenagers in South Sudan" covers too many illnesses.
  A better choice is one illness in an accessible setting: "Characteristics of diabetes mellitus in under 18-year-olds
  in Juba".
- **The literature review** shows what is already known; research **builds on** existing knowledge and does not merely
  replicate it. Librarians may help.
- **Refining the question** after the review gives "Changes in the incidence of type 1 diabetes amongst under-18-year-olds
  in Juba from 2020-2024".
- **Hypothesis:** it can be **statistically rejected if the findings are no more likely than a chance effect**.

### Second example (p. 203)

The study looks at glycaemic control in under-18s with type 1 diabetes, plus patients' **attitudes and behaviour**.
Its question is: "Does contact with health professionals in the diabetes team based at Juba Teaching Hospital from
2022–2023 lead to changes in behaviour and attitude…?" The method compares **before vs after** contact, generates a
hypothesis and tests whether the findings are due to chance. The researcher then picks a quantitative, qualitative
**(or mixed)** approach.

## p. 203–204 – Quantitative research

- **Definition:** "the process of collecting and analysing data. It is widely used to **test hypotheses and causal
  relationships, make predictions**, and find results that can be **generalised** to wider groups of subjects."
- **When to use it (5 bullets):**
  1. To describe the issue in terms of **trends or the relationship among variables**.
  2. To ask research questions that are **specific, narrow, measurable, and observable**.
  3. To collect **numeric data from large numbers of people** using **well-validated instruments**.
  4. To analyse trends, compare groups or relate variables **statistically**, and interpret results against **prior
     predictions and past research**.
  5. To write the report using **standard evaluation criteria**, taking an **objective, unbiased** approach.
- **Researcher's interest:** trends, or **"how one variable affects another"**. The literature review identifies the key
  variables and areas of uncertainty or contradiction.
- **Measurement tool:** its limitations or inaccuracies affect how the results can be interpreted.
- **Sample size** determines the study's **"power"** and its **generalisability**.
- Seek **statistical advice *before*** starting the project.
- **Descriptive statistics** sort and summarise raw data: **mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, quartiles,
  percentiles**. They describe the data *without* inferring a relation between variables.
- **Inferential statistics** draw inferences, e.g. the probability that a difference between groups happened **by
  chance**, and generalise from the sample to a larger population.
- **Sources of bias:**
  - participant **selection**
  - subjects **leaving the study early** (those who remain may differ in important ways)
  - **inappropriate statistical tests**
- **Ways to prevent bias:** **control groups, randomisation, double-blind design, placebos**.
- **Sponsors** may influence how data are presented or even suppress evidence, so researchers must **declare conflicts of
  interest** and financial relationships.

### Types of quantitative research (p. 204)

| Type | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Intervention study** (= **experimental design**, e.g. clinical trial) | One group receives the intervention and is compared with a **control group**. Statistics show whether the results are more than chance and support or disprove the hypothesis. |
| **Correlational study** | A **single group** is studied to find the **relationship between variables**. It can be **prospective (longitudinal)**, measured over time, or **cross-sectional**, at one point in time. |
| – Cross-sectional | **Relatively quick**; used to **determine prevalence**. |
| – Longitudinal | **More likely to suggest cause and effect**, though **hidden variables** may explain the effects. Example: glucose levels vs **diabetic foot ulcers** in a cohort. The strength of the association is tested statistically and used to **predict** the complication in larger, similar populations. |
| **Survey design** | Used when **no intervention is planned and no association is sought**, and the interest is only in **trends**. A **representative sample** is surveyed and the trends are generalised to a larger group. |

- **Advantage:** **objectivity**. Results are **rapidly analysed** and **clearly communicated** through statistics.
- **Limitations:**
  - unsuitable for some types of research
  - participants **cannot enlarge upon their responses or add context**
  - **the patient's perspective is not studied**

  Example: to learn what it is like to live with diabetes, you need a **qualitative** approach.

## p. 204–205 – Qualitative research

- **Definition:** "primarily concerned with **meaning, subjectivity, and lived experience**. It is **exploratory** and
  attempts to understand behaviour **from the perspective of the study participant**."
- **The researcher is integral** to the process, interacting with and interpreting what is said (unlike in
  quantitative research).
- **When to use it (5 bullets):**
  1. To explore a problem and develop a **detailed understanding of a central phenomenon**.
  2. To investigate research relevant to **participants' experiences**.
  3. To collect data **based on words** so that participants' views are obtained.
  4. To analyse for **description and themes** using **textual and thematic analysis**.
  5. To interpret the larger meaning, taking into account the **researchers' subjectivity**.
- **Example methods:** **diary accounts** (written accounts of personal experiences and reflections), **in-depth
  interviews, focus groups, ethnography**.
- **Interviews** can be **unstructured**, using open questions, or **semi-structured**, using predetermined questions
  with the chance to explore responses further.
- **Ethnography** is used in the social and behavioural sciences. Data come from **careful observation**, e.g.
  **shadowing individuals at work** or **living in a particular community**.

| Open-ended questions | Closed-ended questions |
|---|---|
| Qualitative | Quantitative |

### Analysis techniques (p. 205)

- **Thematic analysis:** themes emerge **once the data has been coded**.
  - Figure 2 shows a **continuous cycle**, adapted from **Clarke & Braun, 2017**. Its six steps are:
    1. **Familiarise yourself with the data**: re-read transcripts and notes, write initial ideas.
    2. **Generate initial codes**: highlight or label words and phrases.
    3. **Search for themes**.
    4. **Review potential themes**: make sure they cover all the data.
    5. **Define and name themes**.
    6. **Produce research report**: interpret the data and draw inferences.
  - Themes **can be modified** depending on how well they fit the data.
- **Grounded theory:** collects empirical data (interviews, observations). The researcher looks for themes in the
  transcripts and derives a theory **"grounded" in the results**. It is **not used to test a hypothesis but to generate
  one**.
- **Content analysis:** **quantifies and analyses the presence and relationships of words, themes or concepts** in
  textual data. The researcher then makes inferences about the messages and their meaning for the intended audience.
  It can also be applied to interview transcripts.

## p. 205–206 – Mixed methods research

- The researcher collects **both qualitative and quantitative data**, in some designs **at the same time**.
- **Example:** diabetes care in a **nursing home**. The quantitative data are **glucose readings, HbA1c levels and the
  frequency of hypoglycaemic episodes**; the qualitative data are **interviews with residents**. Together they give a
  **more comprehensive understanding than either method alone**.
- The two approaches **must be integrated**. Integration can happen during **data collection, analysis, or the
  presentation of results**.
- **Planning decisions:** prioritise quantitative or qualitative data, or give both **equal weight**, and plan the
  **sequence** of data collection.

### Figure 3 – Mixed methods designs (p. 205)

| Design | Flow | Purpose (text, p. 206) |
|---|---|---|
| **Convergent parallel** | Quantitative + Qualitative data (simultaneous) → **Compare or relate** → Interpret | Collecting both at the same time |
| **Explanatory sequential** | **Quantitative** → Follow up → **Qualitative** → Interpret | To **explain or elaborate on quantitative results** |
| **Exploratory sequential** | **Qualitative** → Follow up → **Quantitative** → Interpret | To **develop an instrument or tool from qualitative data** |

## p. 206 – Summary

- The two approaches are different but should be seen as **complementary, not competing**.
- **Qualitative research can generate theories or models of care that can then be tested by quantitative methods.**
- The approaches are **not mutually exclusive**. Mixed methods can **deepen understanding of quantitative results**.
- Both help healthcare professionals understand the impact and challenges of the care they provide. **All health care
  team members should be encouraged to participate in research.**
- **Further reading (11 items):** includes Clarke & Braun 2017 (thematic analysis), Schoonenboom & Johnson 2017 (mixed
  methods design), Greenhalgh 2019 (*How to read a paper*), Kirkwood & Sterne 2003, and Spiegelhalter 2019.

## Quick-recall pairs (common MCQ traps)

- Cross-sectional → **quick, prevalence**. Longitudinal/prospective → **more likely to suggest cause and effect**, but
  hidden variables remain possible.
- Intervention study **= experimental design**, which has a control group. A correlational study uses a **single
  group**. A survey uses **no intervention and no association; trends only**.
- Grounded theory **generates** hypotheses and does not test them.
- Content analysis **quantifies** words, themes and concepts in text, even though it is a qualitative technique.
- Explanatory sequential = **QUAN first**, then qual. Exploratory sequential = **QUAL first**, then quan, used to build
  an instrument. Convergent parallel = **both at once**, then compare or relate.
- Sample size → **power** and **generalisability**.
- Descriptive statistics do **not** infer relations between variables. Inferential statistics address **chance** and
  generalise to the population.
- The bias-prevention list has exactly four items: control groups, randomisation, double-blind design, placebos.
  Conflicts of interest relate to sponsors.
- The researcher is **integral** in qualitative research and kept **objective and unbiased** in quantitative research.
- Closed questions are **data-driven, pre-determined, focused**. Open questions are **contextual, personalised,
  exploratory**.
- Figure 1 lists **Narrative** as a qualitative design, alongside grounded theory and ethnography. It lists
  **Experimental, Correlational, Survey** as quantitative designs.
