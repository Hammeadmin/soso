[p.1] Abstract
[p.1] The brewing process generates substantial by-products rich in potentially bioactive com-
[p.1] pounds (e.g., polyphenols and fermentation metabolites), providing a sustainable and
[p.1] appealing source of cosmetic ingredients. Oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions containing 20%
[p.1] (w/w) aqueous extracts from Bionda Triplo Malto beer, wort, and key brewing by-products
[p.1] (hops, yeast, and spent grain) were developed and evaluated using a combined in vitro–
[p.1] in vivo approach. Aqueous extracts were first screened on human immortalized dermal

[p.2] 1. Introduction
[p.2] Skin aging is a multifaceted biological process arising from interactions between
[p.2] intrinsic factors, such as genetic predisposition and cellular senescence, and extrinsic
[p.2] stressors, including ultraviolet (UV) radiation, environmental pollution, and lifestyle-
[p.2] related factors. Many of these alterations primarily target the dermal layer, in which dermal
[p.2] fibroblasts are key regulators of structural integrity and tissue homeostasis. They achieve
[p.2] this by synthesizing extracellular matrix components, maintaining redox homeostasis, and
[p.2] modulating inflammatory and apoptotic signaling pathways. When fibroblast activity is
[p.2] impaired, the skin loses its elasticity, transepidermal water loss (TEWL) increases, and
[p.2] visible signs of aging appear [1–3].
[p.2] Among natural compounds investigated for cosmetic and dermatological applica-
[p.2] tions, polyphenols have attracted considerable attention due to their antioxidant, anti-
[p.2] inflammatory, photoprotective, and anti-aging properties [4–6]. Numerous plant-derived
[p.2] polyphenols have been shown to protect skin cells from oxidative stress, modulate intra-
[p.2] cellular signaling pathways, and preserve dermal matrix integrity, making them valuable
[p.2] ingredients in modern cosmetic formulations [7]. However, increasing evidence suggests
[p.2] that the biological efficacy of polyphenol-rich extracts is strongly influenced by their chem-
[p.2] ical stability, bioavailability, and interaction with the cosmetic vehicle, highlighting the
[p.2] critical role of formulation design [8–10].
[p.2] Recent years have seen a shift in cosmetic industry trends toward sourcing envi-
[p.2] ronmentally conscious ingredients derived from agri-food by-products [11,12]. Brewing
[p.2] by-products are a plentiful and underutilized source of bioactive compounds, including
[p.2] phenolic acids, flavonoids, proteins, polysaccharides, and minerals, that may benefit skin
[p.2] health [11,13,14]. Numerous studies indicate that beer composition and antioxidant ca-
[p.2] pacity are influenced by raw materials, fermentation conditions, and brewing processes,
[p.2] with darker, malt-rich beers typically exhibiting elevated phenolic content and antioxidant
[p.2] potential [15–17]. Additionally, hop- and yeast-derived fractions have been shown to mod-
[p.2] ulate the body’s response to oxidative stress and inflammation, further supporting their
[p.2] potential use in cosmetics [1].
[p.2] Previous work conducted by our team has established that beers and brewing by-
[p.2] products are abundant in phenolic compounds and possess substantial antioxidant capacity,
[p.2] suggesting their potential application as functional ingredients in dietary supplements and
[p.2] cosmetic formulations [18]. Handcrafted beer by-products exhibited anti-aging activity
[p.2] in human keratinocytes, highlighting their potential as cosmetic actives [18]. Triplo Malto
[p.2] beer had the highest phenolic content and antioxidant activity among the beers tested.
[p.2] Based on these findings, the current study aimed to further explore the cosmetic potential
[p.2] of Bionda Triplo Malto beer, its initial wort, and specific brewing by-products (yeast, hops,
[p.2] and spent grain), concentrating on dermal fibroblasts as a pertinent cellular model for skin
[p.2] aging. This study investigates the impact of formulation-driven effects on the cosmetic
[p.2] efficacy of brewing-derived ingredients by integrating in vitro cellular assays with clinical
[p.2] skin measurements, offering new insights into their potential as sustainable and effective
[p.2] cosmetic actives (Figure 1).

[p.3] 2. Materials and Methods
[p.3] 2.1. Materials and Samples
[p.3] Deionized water was produced in-house with a G3 RO CUBIC-S2 demineralizer (Gamma3,
[p.3] Castelverde, Italy). All chemicals were purchased from Merck (Steinheim, Germany).
[p.3] Five different brewing-derived samples were investigated: Bionda Triplo Malto beer
[p.3] (BB), its starting wort (W), and three brewing by-products, namely hops (H), yeast (Y), and
[p.3] spent grain (T), that are residues obtained after the mashing and filtration stages of the beer
[p.3] brewing process. All samples were kindly provided by Birrificio Artigianale Collesi (Apecchio,
[p.3] PU, Italy). Reference compounds with known antioxidant or biological activity were
[p.3] included as controls for in vitro experiments: açai dry extract (previously characterized
[p.3] by our group [19]), Trolox (6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid), and
[p.3] prostaglandin E2 (PGE2).
[p.3] 2.2. Cell Culture
[p.3] Human immortalized dermal fibroblasts BJ-5ta (ATCC® CRL-4001™, male diploid) [20]
[p.3] were used for all in vitro assays. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle
[p.3] Medium/MED 199 (3:1, v/v) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum,
[p.3] penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL) (Life Technologies, Milan, Italy).
[p.3] Cells were maintained at 37 ◦ C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and rou-
[p.3] tinely subcultured at 70–80% confluence.
[p.3] 2.3. Preparation of Aqueous Extracts
[p.3] The extraction procedure was performed under strictly controlled and standardized
[p.3] conditions. The starting materials, beer, hop, wort, yeast, and spent grain, were subjected
[p.3] to different processes according to their physical state, which could be dried solid, humid
[p.3] solid, or turbid liquid, according to a previously developed method [21]. In detail, humid
[p.3] solids and liquids were first subjected to lyophilization at a temperature of −50 ◦ C and a
[p.3] pressure of 0.03 bar (FreeZone 1 Liter Benchtop Series 77400 freeze-dryer, LABCONCO,
[p.3] Kansas City, MO, USA). Dried solids were subjected to milling in a cutter mill. Next, all
[p.3] the samples were subjected to extractions. Each extraction was performed in independent
[p.3] replicates using the same starting materials. A weighed amount was carefully dispersed in
[p.3] water (1 g in 10 mL). The resulting liquid was placed in an Erlenmeyer flask, which was then
[p.3] closed carefully. Samples were magnetically stirred for 24 h at room temperature using a
[p.3] temperature-controlled magnetic stirrer (RCT Basic ETS-D5, IKA, Staufen, Germany), then
[p.3] centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 20 ◦ C for 10 min to remove undissolved particles (Zetalab CNZ-

[p.4] procedure by collecting powders immediately after completion of the freeze-drying cycle,
[p.4] transferring them to airtight containers (polyethylene vials with screw caps, BD Falcon™,
[p.4] BD Biosciences, Bedford, MA, USA), and storing them at −20 ◦ C in 50 mL in order to

[p.4] Extraction yields were calculated as the percentage of recovered dry extract relative to

[p.4] 2.4. Cell Viability Assay (MTS)
[p.4] Cell viability and metabolic activity were evaluated using the MTS assay, based on the
[p.4] reduction of the tetrazolium salt [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-
[p.4] 2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium] to a colored formazan product by metabolically ac-
[p.4] tive cells.
[p.4] BJ-5ta fibroblasts were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates and
[p.4] allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then treated for 24 h with aqueous extracts at
[p.4] concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL as a screening range to evaluate cytocompatibility
[p.4] and potential biological activity under standard culture conditions. Higher concentrations
[p.4] of complex natural matrices may alter medium properties and increase nonspecific assay
[p.4] interference; therefore, the lower range was selected for the primary extract-only evaluation.
[p.4] For experiments involving cosmetic formulations, emulsions containing 20% (w/w)
[p.4] dry extract were diluted in complete culture medium to obtain a final extract concentration
[p.4] of 10 mg/mL. A reference emulsion (Emulsion WE) with identical composition but without
[p.4] extracts was included as a vehicle control. Preliminary controls confirmed the absence of
[p.4] interference between the emulsion matrix and the MTS reagent under the experimental
[p.4] conditions used.
[p.4] Açai extract and Trolox were used as reference antioxidants (0.5 mg/mL), while PGE2
[p.4] was tested at 10−6 M.
[p.4] After treatment, cells were incubated with MTS reagent (20 µL/well) for 1 h at 37 ◦ C.
[p.4] Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite F200, Tecan,
[p.4] Männedorf Switzerland). Recorded values of absorbance at 490 nm were converted to cell
[p.4] viability and expressed as a percentage relative to untreated control cells (control set to
[p.4] 100%) within each experiment.
[p.4] 2.5. Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC) Assay
[p.4] The intracellular antioxidant capacity was evaluated using a commercial Total Antiox-
[p.4] idant Capacity (TAC) colorimetric assay kit (BioVision, K274-100), based on the reduction
[p.4] of Cu2+ to Cu+ by low-molecular-weight antioxidants, with protein interference masked.
[p.4] Fibroblasts were treated with aqueous extracts (0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL) or with
[p.4] cosmetic formulations (10 mg/mL). Oxidative stress was induced using a UVB lamp (peak
[p.4] emission at 312 nm) positioned at a fixed distance from the cell monolayer [22]. Cells were
[p.4] exposed for 5 min under non-lethal conditions, followed by incubation for 24 h prior to
[p.4] TAC measurement. Post-UV cell viability was confirmed by the MTS assay to exclude
[p.4] irradiation-related effects.
[p.4] Cells were incubated for 24 h before TAC measurement, which was performed accord-
[p.4] ing to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 570 nm, and the recorded
[p.4] values were converted to antioxidant capability by means of the Trolox standard calibration
[p.4] curve and expressed as Trolox equivalents.

[p.7] 3. Results
[p.7] 3.1. Extraction Yield
[p.7] For this study, water was selected as the primary extraction solvent over ethanol
[p.7] because previous research [21] showed that ethanol-based extracts did not offer significantly
[p.7] greater biological activity to justify their substantially higher cost. Aqueous extracts were
[p.7] successfully obtained from all brewing-derived materials. Extraction yields were high for
[p.7] beer and wort (approximately 97%), while solid by-products showed yields of 78.0 ± 2.5%,
[p.7] 92.0 ± 5.3%, and 65.0 ± 3.4% for hops, yeast, and spent grain, respectively. These values
[p.7] indicate an efficient recovery of water-soluble components, consistent with the high content
[p.7] of polar bioactive compounds reported for brewing matrices [24].
[p.7] Once prepared, the creams were left to stabilize for 24 h, after which the pH was adjusted
[p.7] to 6.0 with triethanolamine to minimize effects on cells.
[p.7] 3.2. Phytochemical Profiling of the Same Extract Batches Used in This Study (Published Data)
[p.7] For biological interpretation, the brewing-derived extracts tested here—beer, wort,
[p.7] hops, yeast, and spent grain—were prepared from the same production lots previously
[p.7] characterized by our group. In particular, we reported total phenolic content and antiox-
[p.7] idant capacity for these batches using complementary spectrophotometric assays (Folin–
[p.7] Ciocalteu; DPPH/ABTS/FRAP) [21]. We also performed targeted quantitative profiling
[p.7] by a validated HPLC–MS/MS method, measuring 20 representative phenolic compounds
[p.7] (phenolic acids, flavonoids, bitter acids, and prenylflavonoids) across beer, wort, starting
[p.7] ingredients, and spent materials [25].
[p.7] Matrix-dependent compositional differences were observed across these batch-
[p.7] matched datasets. Malt-derived phenolic acids (notably trans-p-coumaric acid) were
[p.7] identified as major contributors in malt and were transferred to wort during mashing,
[p.7] supporting a malt-driven phenolic signature in wort. Conversely, hop-related bitter acids
[p.7] and prenylated flavonoids (e.g., humulone/cohumulone, lupulone/colupulone, xantho-
[p.7] humol/isoxanthohumol, and 8-prenylnaringenin) were characteristic of hop matrices and
[p.7] decreased in spent hops, consistent with transfer and/or processing-related depletion.
[p.7] Spent yeast enriched several phenolic markers, highlighting its capacity to concentrate
[p.7] phenolics from the brewing medium [26].
[p.7] These biological outcomes align with the compositional profiles of the extract batches,
[p.7] with beer- and yeast-based formulations showing stronger effects due to their richer
[p.7] fermentation-derived phenolics and yeast bioactives. To ensure transparency and avoid
[p.7] duplication, readers are referred to the cited studies for full quantitative tables and ana-

[p.8] 3.3. Effect of Brewing-Derived Extracts on Fibroblast Viability
[p.8] A single immortalized human dermal fibroblast cell line (BJ-5ta) was selected for
[p.8] in vitro experiments. This model offers high reproducibility and is suitable for comparative
[p.8] screening of cosmetic formulations, even if it does not fully reflect the cellular complex-
[p.8] ity of human skin. More physiologically relevant systems, such as primary fibroblasts,
[p.8] keratinocytes, or reconstructed skin models, would allow the investigation of epidermal–
[p.8] dermal interactions and barrier-related mechanisms. This limitation is partially offset by
[p.8] an in vivo study in human volunteers, providing clinically relevant confirmation of the
[p.8] formulation-dependent effects seen in vitro.
[p.8] We used the MTS assay to assess fibroblast viability after exposure to aqueous extracts
[p.8] for 24 h at concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL (Figure 3). None of the tested extracts
[p.8] caused statistically significant changes in cell viability compared to untreated control cells
[p.8] at any concentration. A minor, statistically insignificant elevation in metabolic activity was
[p.8] observed in cultures treated with Bionda Triplo Malto beer and wort at a concentration of
[p.8] 0.5 mg/mL. Notably, no cytotoxic effects were detected, confirming the cytocompatibility
[p.8] of all extracts across the tested concentration range. Under the experimental conditions
[p.8] employed, the brewing-derived extracts showed no direct proliferative or cytotoxic effects
[p.8] on dermal fibroblasts, affirming their suitability for cosmetic use.

[p.14] 4. Discussion
[p.14] The growing demand for sustainable, effective, and scientifically validated cosmetic
[p.14] ingredients has stimulated interest in agri-food by-products as sources of bioactive com-
[p.14] pounds. Brewing by-products represent an abundant yet underutilized resource, rich in
[p.14] phenolic compounds, proteins, and other functional molecules with potential benefits for
[p.14] skin health; however, their biological efficacy depends strongly on formulation strategies
[p.14] and experimental conditions.
[p.14] In the current study, aqueous extracts from Bionda Triplo Malto beer, its initial wort,
[p.14] and specific brewing by-products were first assessed in vitro utilizing human immortal-
[p.14] ized dermal fibroblasts. At concentrations up to 1 mg/mL, the extracts did not cause
[p.14] significant changes in cell viability, antioxidant capacity, or the expression of proteins
[p.14] related to apoptosis. These results align with earlier studies showing that polyphenol-rich
[p.14] extracts often exhibit reduced cellular activity when assessed in isolation at moderate
[p.14] concentrations, especially in resilient cell models such as fibroblasts [27–29]. Importantly,
[p.14] the absence of cytotoxic effects confirms the safety profile of these extracts, a key property
[p.14] for cosmetic applications.
[p.14] The results of the TAC assay showed no significant enhancement of intracellular
[p.14] antioxidant defenses following acute UV-induced oxidative stress when free extracts were
[p.14] used. This observation aligns with increasing evidence that antioxidant effects observed in
[p.14] chemical assays do not necessarily translate into cellular efficacy, owing to limited cellular
[p.14] uptake, instability, or rapid metabolism of phenolic compounds [7,30]. These results

[p.15] highlight the intrinsic limitations of testing natural extracts as free agents and underscore
[p.15] the importance and the added value of formulation design in cosmetic research.
[p.15] A key finding of this study was that identical extracts were successfully formulated
[p.15] into oil-in-water cosmetic emulsions at increased effective concentrations. Cosmetic formu-
[p.15] lations, particularly those containing beer (Emu 1) and yeast extracts (Emu 4), significantly
[p.15] enhanced fibroblast viability and antioxidant response compared to free extracts. Incor-
[p.15] porating free extracts at 10 mg/mL into the emulsion matrix fully prevented cell death,
[p.15] highlighting the protective and regulatory role of the cosmetic vehicle.
[p.15] This phenomenon arises from formulation-driven factors such as controlled release,
[p.15] mitigated cellular exposure, and synergy with emollient or emulsifying components. [15,31].
[p.15] The moderate biological activity seen in Emulsion WE supports this theory even more, since
[p.15] cosmetic bases are known to improve skin cell metabolism and barrier-related functions by
[p.15] giving the skin lipids and humectants that act like real skin components [32].
[p.15] Although emulsions improved cellular outcomes (e.g., higher viability and TAC re-
[p.15] sponse versus free extracts at the same nominal active-equivalent concentration), suggesting
[p.15] formulation-driven changes in bioavailability and exposure kinetics, the underlying mech-
[p.15] anisms, such as controlled release, emulsion partitioning, and altered cellular exposure,
[p.15] remain hypothetical without direct measurements. Formulations Emu 1 and 4 consistently
[p.15] outperformed the blank emulsion, demonstrating that the observed effects cannot be at-
[p.15] tributed to the vehicle alone. It is known that yeast-derived ingredients contain β-glucans,
[p.15] peptides, and minerals that can help the skin heal and boost its antioxidant defenses.
[p.15] Beer-derived fractions, on the other hand, contain a complex mix of phenolic acids and
[p.15] fermentation-derived metabolites that work together to have biological effects [26,33,34].
[p.15] The in vivo results strongly corroborate the in vitro findings, representing one of the
[p.15] key strengths of the study. Emulsions containing beer, yeast, and spent grain extracts
[p.15] significantly improved skin barrier function after 30 days of daily application, as shown by
[p.15] lower TEWL, higher hydration, and higher elasticity. These parameters are closely related
[p.15] and are widely regarded as reliable indicators of skin health and anti-aging efficacy [35,36].
[p.15] The reduction in TEWL observed for selected formulations suggests reinforcement of the
[p.15] stratum corneum barrier, consistent with improved hydration and mechanical properties
[p.15] of the skin. As a result, the 20–38% TEWL reductions and ~20–29% increases in hydration
[p.15] observed with beer, yeast, and spent grain emulsions fall within published benchmark
[p.15] ranges, representing meaningful cosmetic improvements rather than marginal effects. The
[p.15] improved biological activity shown in extract-based emulsions relative to free extracts
[p.15] can be explained by the same formulation-mediated mechanisms that are often seen in
[p.15] topical cosmetic vehicles. It is well established that emulsion systems can retain active
[p.15] ingredients and release them slowly and in a controlled manner, rather than all at once,
[p.15] which may reduce acute cytotoxic effects while maintaining biological activity over time.
[p.15] This controlled-release characteristic has been well documented in cosmetic and dermal
[p.15] delivery systems based on emulsions and nanoemulsions. In these systems, the actives are
[p.15] split between the dispersed and continuous phases, which changes how available they are
[p.15] to biological targets [37].
[p.15] In addition, cosmetic emulsions can enhance skin penetration and local bioavail-
[p.15] ability by improving the solubilization of poorly water-soluble compounds, increasing
[p.15] formulation–skin contact, and transiently interacting with the lipid structure of the stratum
[p.15] corneum. These effects have been extensively documented for conventional emulsions,
[p.15] nanoemulsions, and emulgel systems, which promote penetration through intercellular
[p.15] lipid pathways and hydration-mediated barrier modulation [11,14]. Although skin per-
[p.15] meation was not directly assessed in the present study, these mechanisms are relevant for
[p.15] contextualizing the cosmetic applicability of the formulations [38].

[p.16] At the cellular level, formulation vehicles may also influence apparent bioactivity by
[p.16] modulating cellular exposure and uptake. Encapsulation or solubilization of bioactives
[p.16] within colloidal systems can reduce direct membrane damage while facilitating sustained
[p.16] interaction with cells, resulting in improved functional responses compared to free com-
[p.16] pounds at equivalent nominal concentrations. Similar formulation-dependent differences

[p.16] 5. Conclusions
[p.16] The study concludes that beer and selected brewing by-products can serve as sustain-
[p.16] able cosmetic ingredients, provided they are incorporated through appropriate delivery
[p.16] formulations. When tested as free aqueous extracts on dermal fibroblasts at moderate
[p.16] concentrations (0.25–1 mg/mL), all matrices were cytocompatible but did not elicit signifi-
[p.16] cant changes in cellular metabolic activity or intracellular antioxidant capacity following
[p.16] UV-induced stress. In contrast, incorporating the same extracts into oil-in-water emulsions
[p.16] (20% w/w) markedly altered their biological profile: at an active-equivalent concentration
[p.16] of 10 mg/mL, emulsified systems enhanced fibroblast metabolic activity and antioxidant
[p.16] response while mitigating the concentration-dependent effect on viability observed with
[p.16] some free extracts.
[p.16] From a product-development perspective, the emulsions showed a favorable safety
[p.16] and quality profile, including physicochemical stability under accelerated and long-term
[p.16] storage, compliance with preservative efficacy requirements (ISO 11930), and excellent
[p.16] skin tolerability in patch testing. Importantly, an in vivo study in 50 healthy volunteers
[p.16] demonstrated that extract-based emulsions, especially those with beer, yeast, and spent
[p.16] grain, enhanced clinically relevant skin parameters over 30 days, reducing TEWL and
[p.16] increasing hydration and elasticity.
[p.16] Overall, these findings highlight that the efficacy of complex natural matrices in
[p.16] cosmetics depends not only on the activity of free extracts but also on formulation-driven
[p.16] modulation of bioavailability and exposure kinetics. The present work supports brewing-
[p.16] derived matrices as functional cosmetic actives, aligning with circular-economy principles.
[p.16] Future studies should include post-treatment follow-up to assess the persistence of benefits
[p.16] and incorporate mechanistic endpoints (e.g., penetration, uptake, and pathway-specific
[p.16] oxidative stress markers) to better substantiate formulation-driven effects.
